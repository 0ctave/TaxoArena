\chapter{Reference-Informed Judge Generation}\label{app:judge-generation}

This appendix details the rubric-generation mechanics referenced in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental-design}.

\section{Generation Procedure}

The judge rubric generation procedure (Section~\ref{sec:arch-judge}) is
instantiated as follows. A leaf's induction pool is its construction
queries \emph{after} two filters: the reserved-set filter, which is audited
by a build-failing assertion and is what makes the answer-key-blind claim
enforceable rather than asserted, and a hash filter that thins the pool by
roughly a fifth.

That pool is used in full. \texttt{TaxonomyJudgeService.generateJudgeForNode}
chunks it into batches of $25$ (\texttt{details.chunked(25)}), induces partial
guidelines from \emph{every} batch, and synthesises them into one rubric. The
$25$ is a batch size set by prompt length, not a sample size: a leaf's rubric is
induced from all of its filtered construction queries, not from a representative
subset of them.
The map-step LLM and the reduce-step LLM are the same underlying model
as the arena judge, to avoid introducing a second
model's stylistic biases into the rubric. That choice has a cost, and
it is stated here because no other section carries it: inducing the
rubric with the model that will later apply it bakes the judge's own
priors into the criteria, which weakens the rubric-against-generic
contrast of Section~\ref{sec:res-c5}. A judge that agrees
with a rubric written by itself is a weaker test than a judge that
agrees with a rubric written by someone else. Guideline generation is
a one-time cost per frozen snapshot: rubrics are computed once at
snapshot-freeze time and reused for every subsequent arena run against
that snapshot, regardless of which models are being compared.

\section{Structural Template}\label{sec:judge-template-full}

The global structural template, covering presentation order, tie
handling, and output format, is fixed across all leaves. It governs
only the shared scaffolding of the judge prompt; the leaf-specific
reference-informed content (representative queries, answer-key
context) is injected into this template at generation time and does
not modify its structure.

The separation the template enforces is what makes the two arms
comparable, so it is stated explicitly. The induced, leaf-specific half
defines \emph{what} to evaluate; the fixed half defines \emph{how}. The
synthesis step that produces each leaf's judge
(\texttt{JudgePrompts.synthesizeFinalJudge}) is instructed in these
terms, and the instruction is quoted rather than paraphrased because
the constraint it imposes is the thesis's control:

\begin{quote}\small
``This persona defines WHAT to evaluate (domain knowledge, axioms,
failure modes) --- NOT HOW to evaluate. Verdict format, tie policy, and
evaluation steps are controlled externally and must NOT appear here.''
\end{quote}

\noindent It further requires each rubric to ``[s]pecialise strictly''
to its leaf and to ``ignore generic qualities that do not affect
technical correctness: formatting, markdown, response length, stylistic
elegance, model identity'', and forbids any ``compare A vs B'', ``pick
a winner'', or tie-break instruction from entering the induced text.
Verdict format is a JSON object with exactly two string keys,
\texttt{system\_prompt} and \texttt{rubric}.

The generic-rubric arm (C5) replaces the induced half with the MT-Bench
pairwise judge prompt verbatim (\texttt{GenericPairwiseJudgePrompt}).

\paragraph{The two arms differ in more than the rubric.} This is a limitation of
the contrast and is stated here because Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports a
measured difference between the arms. The generic arm is not the rubric arm with
its system prompt swapped: it is a separate code path
(\texttt{TaxonomyArenaService}, the \texttt{isC5} branch), and it differs in four
respects at once.

The consequence is that any measured difference between the arms is attributable
to the bundle, not to the rubric alone. The reasoning scaffold runs in the
direction opposite to the observed effect --- the rubric arm is the one that
forces a four-step critique before a verdict --- so scaffolding is unlikely to
explain a generic-arm advantage, but the output format and the fixed confidence
are not controlled. Isolating the rubric would require running the MT-Bench
system prompt through the rubric arm's own user template and parser, which was
not done.

Under the replay design of Section~\ref{sec:exp-rqmap}, the two arms see the
same comparison set by construction. Measured overlap is $100\%$ on
mathematics, physics, psychology and philosophy, and $99.6\%$ on law and
engineering, where a handful of replays failed and were not retried.

\section{What the Judge Prompt Contains}\label{sec:judge-prompt-contents}

The mechanism behind this thesis's central RQ1 finding is visible in the
prompt, so what it does and does not contain is stated explicitly. In every
mode the judge receives the leaf's induced rubric as a system prompt, the
question stem, and the two stored responses; it never receives the MMLU-Pro
reference answer. The prompt-construction path asserts that the rendered query
carries no ground-truth answer block, and the stored correct answer is never
read on the judging path.

What varies is the option context.

\begin{description}
\item[\texttt{OPTIONS}] appends the full multiple-choice options block to the
stem.
\item[\texttt{RESOLVED}] withholds the options block and instead attaches each
model's own selected option to its trace as text, so a verdict can refer to an
answer rather than to a bare letter. This is the settled batch's mode.
Attachment succeeded on $93.2\%$ to $99.8\%$ of traces by domain; the shortfall
is traces whose upstream \texttt{pred} field is missing, which are passed
through verbatim.
\end{description}

The measured consequence is that ``answer-key-blind'' is a much weaker property
than ``answer-blind''. Under either mode a judge able to solve the item can
verify both responses against its own solution without ever being told the key,
and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports that this is what it does. Withholding the
options block narrows the channel but does not close it, because the responses
themselves state their answers.

\section{Rubric Reach: The Continuous Analysis}\label{sec:app-rubric-reach}

Demoted from Section~\ref{sec:res-offsubject}, which keeps the
headline numbers. Measured as cosine distance to the centroid of the
queries its rubric was induced from, a routed held-out question sits
at $0.511$ --- $38\%$ of the way from a random reserved question
($0.607$) toward construction-grade proximity ($0.353$), and only
$6$--$21\%$ of the way in five of eight domains. The premise of the
rubric-coverage worry is therefore real: rubrics are applied well
outside the material they were induced from. The consequence is not:
within cells, one standard deviation of that distance moves the
cell-scoped arm's tie rate by $+1.02$ percentage points --- and moves
the \emph{generic} arm, which carries no domain criteria at all, by
$+1.06$. The within-triple difference is $-0.04$ points
($95\%$ CI $[-1.39, +1.30]$, cell fixed effects, $n = 2{,}788$).

\section{Dual-Call Position-Reversal Protocol}

Each logical comparison is realised as two judge calls with response
position reversed between calls, per the dual-call protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:arch-judge}. Disagreement between the two calls is
resolved as a tie, rather than by a coin flip or by discarding the
comparison. How that tie is then scored is a reporting convention and
not a modelling choice: every rank correlation appears under both
half-weighting and dropping. Making tie propensity a fitted parameter,
as \textcite{davidson1970ties} does, is the alternative this system
does not implement and is named as the principled repair
(Section~\ref{subsec:disc-construct-validity}).

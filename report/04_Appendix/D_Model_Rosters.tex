\chapter{Model Rosters and Exclusion Policy}\label{app:models}

Model identifiers are given exactly as passed to the arena (provider-side API
or checkpoint names). One roster carries every result in this thesis: the
eight-model roster of the settled batch. The earlier rosters are recorded here
as history --- their campaigns are withdrawn, and no number in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} rests on them (Section~\ref{sec:exp-rosters}).

\paragraph{Why eight, and why this spread.} The roster was cut from twelve to
eight to buy \emph{decidability}. Whether the answer key can separate two models
at $N$ routed questions depends on the gap between their accuracies, and a
roster of closely matched models leaves most pairs unresolvable at any budget
this thesis can afford (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}). Spanning $16.5\%$ to
$90.5\%$ --- a factor of $5.5$ --- makes $24$ to $28$ of the $28$ pairs
decidable in every domain, which is what allows Chapter~\ref{ch:results} to
report rank recovery against the key at all.

\paragraph{Three properties bound what this roster may claim.} They are stated
here because the accuracy spread that buys decidability is bought back in other
coin.

\emph{The roster is not length-matched.} Median stored output runs from $220$ to
$1{,}494$ characters, a factor of $6.79$. The earlier twelve-model roster was
assembled specifically to hold this ratio to $2.91$, and that control has been
given up. Any residual verbosity preference in the judge is therefore free to
correlate with capability here, and the thesis cannot separate the two from
these runs alone. This is a real limitation of the settled batch and is not
repaired by the option-injection protocol, which changes what the judge is told
about the answer rather than how long the response is.

\emph{\texttt{arx\_0314} answers bare on $21.4\%$ of items} and is of unknown
provenance --- plausibly retrieval-augmented or an ensemble. Rows involving it
support arena claims but not claims about base LLM capability, and its
format inconsistency reintroduces, inside its own comparisons, the preference
the exclusion policy below exists to remove. It sits under the policy's
operative threshold rather than clear of it.

\emph{\texttt{gemini-3.1-pro\_5-shots} is a five-shot prompting condition} in an
otherwise zero-shot roster, and is labelled as such wherever it appears. It is
also the strongest model in the roster, so the prompting advantage and the
capability ranking are confounded at the top.

\section{The Earlier Rosters}\label{sec:roster-earlier}

Two earlier rosters belong to the withdrawn run generations and are
recorded here as history, not as sources of any number. A
\textbf{twelve-model length-matched band} (seven families, at most two
per family) carried the withdrawn paired campaign; it was selected to
control the response-length artifact --- median response length
$486$--$1{,}413$ characters ($2.91\times$), length--accuracy
correlation $r = +0.286$ inside the roster against $+0.597$ across all
$36$ valid models --- and that control is the one property of it the
settled selection gave up. The \textbf{pilot roster} of eight models
carried the capture-gap defect, and Table~\ref{tab:model-roster-pilot}
preserves the one thing it uniquely demonstrates: response format and
capability were perfectly collinear, which is why format could decide
half of that run's comparisons and why the exclusion policy below
tests format consistency.

\section{The Exclusion Policy}\label{sec:roster-exclusion}

Ten models are excluded at roster load by a hard-coded list that raises, each
entry carrying its measured reason: one identifier-space mismatch, six archives
with no stored reasoning text, and three mixed-format models that emit a bare
answer on $68.4\%$, $35.4\%$ and $27.6\%$ of items. The operative threshold is
format \emph{consistency}, not text presence --- a model that answers bare on a
third of items reintroduces the format preference inside its own comparisons.
The policy is live: it aborted a candidate roster for the settled batch.

Three notes belong with it.

\textbf{One name is not the other.} \texttt{Meta-Llama-3\_1-70B-Instruct} is in
the settled roster and is clean. \texttt{Meta-Llama-3-70B-Instruct} is excluded,
sits in a different question-identifier space, and overlaps the other models'
law questions on exactly one item; its presence in an intersection is what
collapsed law from $287$ questions to $20$ and inverted a domain recommendation
(Section~\ref{sec:res-law}).

\textbf{The policy does not generalise.} Six of the ten exclusions are for
exactly the condition the corpus validator's trace-presence check measures and
declines to act on, because that check has no failure branch. The policy is a
ratchet on known failures, not a threshold, and a new model sharing the defect
would be admitted --- which is how \texttt{arx\_0314}, at $21.4\%$ bare, sits in
the settled roster. The general guard is a one-line change and is named as
future work (Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}).

\textbf{The judge is in no roster.} The judge model is
\texttt{Mistral-Large-3} in every reported run, verified against the runtime
configuration and the run logs rather than read off a design document, two of
which named different models. It appears in none of the rosters above, so there
is no self-preference confound in any reported run. No constraint enforces
this; it is a property of these runs.

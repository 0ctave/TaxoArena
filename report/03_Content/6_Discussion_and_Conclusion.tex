\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Where the Architecture Binds}\label{sec:disc-rq1}

Section~\ref{sec:res-judge}.
MMLU-Pro is multiple-choice with a verifiable answer key. In the settled
runs the judge is not shown the options block, but $97.6\%$ of the stored
traces state the answering model's own selected option
(Section~\ref{sec:arch-judge}). A judge capable of
solving the item can therefore verify both responses against its own
solution and decide the comparison without consulting the rubric at
all --- while still being answer-key-blind in the sense the protocol
guarantees, because nothing in the prompt tells it which option is
right.

The evidence that this is what it does is the five measurements of
Section~\ref{sec:res-judge-summary}, two of them matched controls, all
pointing the same way. The shortcut is partial, and the split of
Section~\ref{sec:res-competence} names the boundary: the judge
shortcuts to correctness where it can check the item ($91.1\%$
agreement where the correct model is the pair's stronger), and falls
back on the candidates' reputation where it cannot ($56.5\%$ where the
correct model is the pair's weaker). What the rubric was supposed to
supply is displaced either way --- by verification on one side of the
boundary, by a competence prior on the other.

The consequence for the architecture is precise and it is not a
failure. \textbf{The partition can only bind where correctness is not
checkable.} On open-ended generation, on agent traces, on tasks with no
key --- the settings the routing motivation of
Section~\ref{sec:motivation} is actually about --- the judge has no
shortcut available and the rubric is the only thing standing between it
and an undocumented heuristic. On a multiple-choice corpus it has a
shortcut, and it takes it.

The two halves of that statement belong together. MMLU-Pro grounds the
comparison against ground truth, and it is simultaneously the corpus on
which the judging mechanism can bypass what the comparison is about.
The first is why the arena could be validated at all. The second is why
validating it there does not establish the thing the architecture was
built for.

This is an inference from the settled eight-domain batch, not a
demonstration, and it is replicated rather than isolated: the five
measurements behind it (Section~\ref{sec:res-judge-summary}) hold in
every domain, but all of them sit on the middle rung of the
options-mode ladder, where the responses still state their own
answers. The demonstration is the registered options-blind arm ---
the same questions and the same rubric with both the options and the
stated answers withheld --- whose predictions are already fixed in
writing and which is blocked on a trace-complete roster
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}).

\section{What Each Negative Means, Link by Link}\label{sec:disc-rq2}

This thesis reports several negative results, and they
do not mean the same thing, because they land on different links of the
chain in Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}.

\textbf{``Cells do not carry different true rankings'' lands on link 3's
premise.} It is a statement about MMLU-Pro and about a roster spanning
$74$ accuracy points: cell identity adds essentially nothing to model
ordering beyond global capability. At the settled roster the finding
is exceptionless --- no domain and no individual leaf pair, zero of
$293$, survives correction (Section~\ref{sec:res-leaf-null}). At the
superseded, more clustered roster a small exception existed (three
mathematics leaf pairs separating continuous from discrete
mathematics), which locates the boundary: sub-structure becomes
expressible only when models sit close together. The finding says
nothing at all about whether cell-scoped judging produces better
judgments, because it never touches a verdict; it is computed on
ground-truth accuracy alone.

\textbf{This is also what bounds any positive result.}
Cell identity adds almost nothing to model ordering, so no arm contrast
on this corpus could have been evidence that cell-scoped judging
\emph{discovers} capability the domain-level arm misses. There is little
such capability here to discover. The direct measurement is inside law, a
domain the arena actually ran: at the settled roster, each of law's six
cells' ground-truth ranking agrees with the domain ranking as well as
the ground truth agrees with itself; the figures are quoted once, in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\textbf{``The rubric does not change the verdict'' lands on link 3's
mechanism.} And it is not a parity finding. On the settled batch the
two arms differ only in the rubric bundle --- the generic arm replays
the cell-scoped arm's own triples under the same cells
(Appendix~\ref{app:preregs}) --- and they reach the same ranking in
seven of eight domains and within one adjacent transposition in the
eighth, disagreeing on individual verdicts often enough to be
measurable (the decisiveness contrast of Section~\ref{sec:res-c5})
but never enough to reorder. Two arms that close cannot produce a
detectable ranking difference at any sample size; no amount of power
repairs a comparison whose treatment is nearly the control.
The transferable consequence is a precondition rather than a result.
Measure verdict agreement before spending a judging budget on an A/B
arm.

\textbf{Neither touches links 1 or 2, and both of those came out
positive.} The construction reaches a certified fixed point and
produces cells of the intended size distribution; rubrics induced on
those cells are specific to them against a size-matched randomised
null, with all four pre-registered discriminators firing in the
predicted directions. The chain does not
break at the partition. It breaks at the step where a better rubric was
supposed to produce a better verdict, on a corpus where the verdict was
already decided by something else.

\textbf{And the reading rule matters more than the reading.} A null
under a decision rule fixed before the data is a finding. A null under a
rule chosen afterwards is a shrug. Every negative above was read
against a criterion recorded in a dated document: three outcomes named
in advance for the granularity analysis, with ``observed $\rho$ is
primary'' fixed in advance --- and that mattered, because the
disattenuated column moves the opposite way and clips past $1.0$, so
leading with it would have produced the opposite conclusion from the
arithmetic of the correction rather than from the data. Four
directional predictions with the confound direction named per measure
for the rubric null. A decisive difference of two Spearman grid steps,
and four void conditions, fixed for the rubric-specificity baseline
before its verdicts existed. That decisive difference has since been
withdrawn (Section~\ref{sec:exp-metrics}); the void conditions stand.

A reader must not be allowed to collapse ``cells do not carry different
true rankings'' into ``the taxonomy does not help the arena''. The first
is a property of this corpus's saturation for this roster. The second is
a claim about judge quality, and the measurement that would decide it
has not been run in the only regime where the rubric has been shown able
to matter.

\section{The Apparatus Caught Its Own Errors}\label{sec:disc-selfcheck}

Five instances. None of the following was found by
looking for it, which is why they are the best available evidence that
the results the apparatus did \emph{not} overturn are sound.

\textbf{The response-capture gap} was found while sizing the roster for
a different experiment. It showed that half of the pilot arena's
comparisons had been decided by which side had text at all, and it
qualified figures that were already written down
(Section~\ref{sec:res-capture-gap}).

\textbf{A side-channel in two candidate roster entries} was found by a
pre-launch format check whose only anomalous column was newline count:
the two systems' stored output was a raw JSON envelope carrying a
self-reported code that predicts the very outcome being judged.
Neither system entered any arena run as stored; the full account is in
Appendix~\ref{app:corrections-register}.

\textbf{The reliability constant} was caught twice, by two independent
routes that compounded to the same factor: a re-fit at a different
roster size, and a units check on a split-half length mismatch
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-debt}).

\textbf{The domain screen's two reversals} both traced to one cause,
found only because the screen was re-run at the roster the arena would
actually use (Section~\ref{sec:res-law}).

\textbf{The first evaluative-diversity null had no valid comparison
arm}, and this is the most instructive of the five because the error
was in the null rather than in the treatment. Pseudo-leaves were
sampled independently, which gave them 11--26\% pairwise question
overlap where real leaves have 1.6--4.9\%. Overlapping pseudo-leaves
agree with each other for free, so the null was inflated and the real
cells looked distinctive against it. Matching the overlap reverses the
answer (Section~\ref{sec:res-leaf-null}). A comparison arm is a
measurement and has to be checked like one.

Two design features did the work in most of these cases, and both are
cheap. A check whose only informative column is an unglamorous one,
newline count, caught what length, citation rate and URL rate did not.
And re-deriving a statistic at the configuration the decision will
actually run at caught both screen reversals.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}\label{sec:disc-validity}

\subsection{Internal Validity}\label{subsec:disc-internal-validity}

The information separation that the answer-key-blind claim rests on is
enforced in code and audited by a build-failing assertion
(Section~\ref{sec:arch-judge}), which is the strongest guarantee in the
system. It does not, however, make the judge answer-\emph{blind}, and
Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq1} is the consequence.

Two internal threats are unmitigated. Comparisons are not independent
given the Bradley--Terry parameters, because a single held-out query is
reused across many pairs; the Fisher standard errors treat them as
independent and therefore understate uncertainty by an unmeasured
margin. And the scheduler over-samples uncertain pairs, which biases
win-rate (Section~\ref{sec:meth-scheduling}); the Bradley--Terry
strength $\theta$ is quoted throughout.

\subsection{External Validity}\label{subsec:disc-external-validity}

Every arena result in this thesis is on one corpus, at seed 42, on
eight of the corpus's fourteen domains, seven of them complete, with
rosters of 8 to 12 models. The construction is
contingent on one embedding model: if the embedding changes, the
snapshot is invalid and results produced under different embeddings are
not comparable. The anchor structure is seeded from MMLU-Pro's
editorial domains and thereafter immutable, so anchor-level results are
rankings conditioned on MMLU-Pro's partitioning; cross-domain migration
mitigates this at the query level but cannot relocate or merge anchors.
Discovery is bounded by the corpus available at construction time,
and there is no incremental insertion mechanism --- a new corpus
requires a full reconstruction.

The roster exclusion policy is a further external-validity limit. Ten
models are excluded by a hard-coded list of names, each with a
recorded reason; the policy is a ratchet on known failures, not a
threshold, and it does not generalise to any new model
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-rosters}). A general guard is a one-line change
and is named as future work.

\subsection{Construct Validity}\label{subsec:disc-construct-validity}

\textbf{Judge-side benchmark contamination.}
MMLU-Pro is a public benchmark, and its questions and answer keys
plausibly appear in the pre-training corpora of contemporary
LLMs~\parencite{deng2024contamination}; contamination-limited benchmark
designs exist precisely because the problem is
pervasive~\parencite{white2024livebench}.
The specific threat here is not that the \emph{evaluated} models saw
the questions --- that affects the accuracy oracle and the arena
symmetrically --- but that the \emph{judge} may recognise a held-out
question and its answer from training. A memorising judge approximates
the accuracy oracle directly, and would inflate rank agreement for
reasons unrelated to the evaluation mechanism. The answer-key-blind
protocol does not defend against it, because the information sits in
the judge's weights rather than in the prompt.

The generic-judge and generic-rubric arms discipline this threat only
partially.
Because all arms share the same judge model, contamination contributes
to each alike and is differenced out of any contrast between them --- so
a rubric gain that survived the control could not be explained by
memorised answer keys. But the measured contrast is approximately zero
(Section~\ref{sec:res-c5}), so there is
no gain for the control to protect. What the control establishes is
therefore the absence of an effect rather than the
contamination-resilience of a present one. The absolute agreement levels remain an upper bound on
mechanism-attributable validity, and a direct estimate of judge
memorisation (verdict consistency under paraphrase, or a probe set
published after the judge's training cutoff) is future work.

\textbf{Residual judge bias.}
The dual-call protocol converts positional disagreement into an
explicit tie, and a post-hoc adjustment rewrites a pair's counts when
its order asymmetry is large (Section~\ref{sec:arch-judge}). Verbosity
bias~\parencite{saito2023verbosity} is not mitigated: the rubric
contains no length-normalisation criterion, and the capture-gap result
of Section~\ref{sec:res-capture-gap} is an extreme instance of a
length-adjacent preference --- on mixed pairs the judge takes the side
with text on $863/868$ of comparisons. The settled roster makes this
sharper rather than softer: its median stored response runs from $220$
to $1{,}494$ characters, a factor of $6.79$, where the superseded
twelve-model roster was assembled to hold that ratio to $2.91$
(Appendix~\ref{app:models}). Response length and model capability are
therefore confounded in these runs and cannot be separated from them. Self-preference
bias~\parencite{panickssery2024llm} does not arise here, because the
judge model appears in no roster,\footnote{Verified rather than
assumed: the judge is Mistral-Large-3 in every reported run, confirmed
against the Run~B run log (four occurrences of the judge identifier,
none of any smaller ``ministral'' variant) and the runtime
configuration; no roster contains it (Appendix~\ref{app:models}).}
but no constraint enforces that; it
is a property of these runs rather than of the system.

\textbf{Tie approximation.}
Ties are encoded as half-wins inside the standard Bradley--Terry
likelihood, without a tie-propensity parameter. This is not a harmless
approximation. Section~\ref{sec:res-tie-policy} shows the choice of tie
convention deciding which arm appears ahead on one paired contrast, and
law and engineering both change verdict shape between the two
conventions, which is why both are always reported. Fitting tie
propensity as a parameter, as Davidson
does~\parencite{davidson1970ties}, would replace the convention with an
estimate and is the principled repair.

\subsection{The Standing Measurement Debt}\label{sec:disc-debt}

Two of the following are errors this thesis found in its own
previously reported numbers. They are presented under the frame of
Section~\ref{sec:disc-selfcheck}. The full
account of each --- the derivation, the pre-correction values, and
what was withdrawn --- is told once, in the corrections register of
Appendix~\ref{app:corrections-register}; no interval computed before
the corrections is quoted anywhere in this document. What remains here
is the corrected state and where each correction binds.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Bradley--Terry standard errors.} The rank completion
requires subtracting $1/K^2$ after inversion; the implementation
subtracted $1/K$, so no pre-correction standard error is a
measurement --- $45$ of the pilot run's $176$ stored values sit at the
$0.01$ floor and the rest are understated, against corrected values 44
to 209 times larger (Section~\ref{sec:meth-bt}). No pre-correction
interval or ``resolved pair'' determination is quoted. The paired runs
from law onward postdate the fix and carry measured standard errors;
across the first five of them, $288$ leaf-level values with a median of
$0.31$ to $0.48$ by domain, a minimum of $0.149$, and none at the
floor.

\item \textbf{The scheduler consequence of that bug, and which runs it
touches.} The fix is dated,\footnote{Commit \texttt{a92a3ed},
2026-07-27.} and Runs~A and~B predate it. Their schedulers consumed standard errors that
were floored for about a quarter of nodes and understated for the rest,
with three likely mechanical consequences: the variance division inside
$U_{\text{resolve}}$ degenerated toward a constant, so the term
approached pure entropy; the maturity-driven mixing weight $\alpha$,
keyed to falling per-leaf standard errors, did not mature as designed;
and the $3\sigma$ resolve rule tended toward a fixed margin
$|\hat{s}_i - \hat{s}_j| \geq 0.06$. The allocation those runs produced
is therefore closer to entropy-driven than to the variance-weighted
design of Section~\ref{sec:meth-scheduling}, and no reading of either
may lean on adaptive allocation. The paired runs from law onward
postdate the fix and ran with measured standard errors.

\item \textbf{The reliability constant and the disattenuation form.}
The published $c = 7.66$ corrects to $c = 2.52$ --- a split-half
length mismatch compounded with a roster re-fit --- and disattenuation
is two-sided, $\rho / r$ rather than $\rho / \sqrt{r}$
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-metrics}). The correction compresses the
reliability ladder across granularities from a spread of $0.219$ to
$0.088$, so leaf-scale cells stop being individually low-reliability;
that weakens the reliability leg of the argument for judging at a
coarser cut, and the identification-cost result of
Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} is untouched and probably carries that
argument alone.

\item \textbf{The evaluative-redundancy percentages} could not be
reproduced under either an observed-primary or a disattenuated
reading and are withdrawn; no redundancy percentage is asserted in
this thesis.

\item \textbf{One convention-dependent figure}, the correctness-blind
rank correlation (Section~\ref{sec:res-correctness}), is reported with
its governing convention named where it appears. A second, the leaf-level between-cell
agreement quoted as $0.922$ against $0.884$, is withdrawn. The $0.922$
of Table~\ref{tab:granularity} is measured on each cell's own
construction assignments; a separate $0.922$ measured on re-routed
assignments does not reproduce, and no claim in this thesis rests on
it.

\item \textbf{Routing expected calibration error} is implemented and
was exported at $0.2114$, but under a mis-specified aggregation that
understates every confidence by a known sign and an unknown magnitude.
The full statement is in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}, where the metric
is defined; the exported value is quoted only as a diagnostic and no
claim rests on it.

\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis set out to validate a taxonomy and ended by measuring a
judge. Restating the contributions of
Section~\ref{sec:research-goal} as what was demonstrated rather than
what was attempted:

\textbf{What was demonstrated.}
On a corpus with a verifiable answer key, an LLM judge's pairwise
verdicts are produced jointly by answer verification and a competence
prior, not by reasoning quality. Five
measurements establish it, all on the settled batch, two of them
matched controls, each by a distinct mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
\item recovery of the key's ordering on $196$ of the $200$ model pairs
the key can separate --- one shared ordering, recovered eight times on
disjoint question samples --- with agreement on individual
discriminable comparisons of $86\%$ where the judge commits to a
winner;
\item a tie rate that nearly doubles ($13.0\%$ to $24.0\%$) exactly
where the key stops discriminating, in all eight domains;
\item agreement that splits on candidate strength --- $91.1\%$ where
the correct model is the pair's stronger against $56.5\%$ where it is
the weaker --- locating where verification ends and the prior takes
over;
\item off-subject questions judged at the same agreement rate as
on-subject ones, so the cell rubric's domain criteria are not what
decides --- a control that varies the material;
\item and a cell-scoped against generic rubric contrast, replaying
identical comparisons, that changes the judge's abstention rate
($13.0\%$ against $10.9\%$) and not its ranking --- a control that
varies the prompt.
\end{itemize}
A construction method was built and characterised as an instrument: it
reaches a certified structural fixed point, its acceptance rule is
calibrated in units of its own objective's paired standard error, and
its separation threshold is positioned between two measured nulls
(Section~\ref{sec:res-construction}). The acceptance rule is adopted on
coherence alone: both of the things one would naturally cite in its
support, that it tightens run-to-run stability and that it raises the
objective it gates, were measured and refuted.
Rubrics induced on its cells were shown to be specific to those cells
against a size-matched randomised null at
$p = 1.21 \times 10^{-6}$, with all four pre-registered discriminators
firing in their predicted directions.
And the premise of this thesis's original second research question ---
that cells carry different true model rankings --- was tested and
refused under pre-registration, before the arena ran.

\textbf{Answering the research questions.}
RQ1 asked whether answer-key-blind, domain-conditioned pairwise
judgments recover per-domain capability rankings, and what decides the
verdicts. On the settled batch they recover the key's ordering on
$196$ of the $200$ model pairs the key can separate, and what decides
the verdicts is answer correctness: agreement with the key where the
judge commits to a winner, a tie rate that nearly doubles exactly
where the key stops discriminating, and off-subject questions judged
at on-subject rates, each quantified in the list above
(Section~\ref{sec:res-judge-summary}).

RQ2 asked whether adapted, cell-scoped grouping enables better judging
than a generic rubric on the same questions. It does not change what
the judging concludes: replaying the cell-scoped arm's exact
comparisons under a generic MT-Bench prompt produces the same ranking
in seven of eight domains and one adjacent transposition in the
eighth. What it changes is decisiveness --- the cell-scoped arm
declines to name a winner more often, in all eight domains, at the
rates in the list above --- and that difference is attributable
to the judging condition as a bundle, not to the rubric text alone,
because verdict format and confidence handling travel with the prompt
(Section~\ref{sec:res-c5}). The isolation run that would attribute it
is future work.

The domain selection was fixed under the offline screen's
since-superseded fit. Refit at the settled roster the screen
\emph{saturates} --- the domain ordering
equals the global ordering in twelve of fourteen domains --- so the
eight domains are read as replications of one measurement, and the
screen classifies a domain aggregate without touching the
within-domain question (Section~\ref{sec:res-law}).

One bound travels with every positive above and is not optional. What a
positive gap buys is a less noisy estimate of a ranking the two arms
already share, not the discovery of capability the domain arm misses.
On law, at the settled roster, per-cell ground-truth rankings agree
with the domain ranking at Spearman $0.929$ to $1.000$, against a
split-half range of $0.922$ to $1.000$ for ground truth against
itself, and a permutation null finds no domain with evaluative
sub-structure at all.

Mathematics was registered in advance as a null arm, and whether that
prediction holds is now open rather than answered: the run that answered it
is superseded (Appendix~\ref{app:preregs}).

\textbf{What the scope licenses, and what it does not.}
These are results about one multiple-choice corpus, one judge model,
eight of its fourteen domains with seven complete, rosters of 8 to 12
models, and a single seed. They
license the claim that on a corpus of this kind the judging mechanism
can bypass the partition, and they license the negative result about
geometry-adapted evaluation partitioning for a roster of this
capability spread. They do \emph{not} license a claim that cell-scoped
rubrics fail to improve judging in general: the regime in which a
rubric has been shown able to matter --- where correctness is not
checkable --- has not been tested here. Nor do they license any
absolute rank-fidelity figure quoted to three decimals
(Section~\ref{sec:res-tie-policy}); only paired contrasts within a run
are reported as such.

\textbf{What the reframing contributes.}
The evaluative value of a partition is a claim about a judge's
generalisation surface, and it decomposes into three separately
falsifiable links: coherent cells, specific rubrics, better judgments.
Stated that way, this project's negative results become locatable:
link~1 holds, link~2 holds, and link~3 fails at its premise on this
corpus and at its mechanism on this judging regime. Stated the other
way, as a single claim that adapted partitioning improves ranking
fidelity, the same results read as a project that did not work. The
decomposition is not a post-hoc rescue; it was written down and dated
before the runs it now organises.

\section{Outlook and Future Directions}\label{sec:disc-future}

In priority order, with the reason each sits where it does.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{A rule-identifier citation scheme in the verdict schema.}
Section~\ref{sec:res-c5} shows the cell rubric does not change the
judge's ranking, but no lexical analysis can show whether the judge
\emph{applies} a rubric's criteria at all. Requiring the verdict to
name the rule it relied on settles that
directly --- a cited rule identifier cannot arise from topical
coincidence --- and it counts, for free, how many verdicts cite no rule
at all and are therefore decided on priors. It is first on this list
because it changes the verdict schema and therefore must precede any
further arena run.

\item \textbf{The options-blind arm.} The same held-out questions, the
same cell rubrics, with the multiple-choice options withheld from the
judge. This is the demonstration that Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq1}
currently has only as an inference, and its predictions are already
fixed in writing per stratum. It is \emph{blocked} until the roster is
trace-complete: without options and without stored reasoning, an
untraced-against-untraced comparison presents the judge with a question
and two bare letters, which is uninterpretable rather than a
chance-level null. The corpus repair of
Section~\ref{sec:res-capture-gap} removes that block for most of the
corpus, and re-running generation with output capture removes it for
the rest.

\item \textbf{The six domains never run, and what running them would
now mean.} Six of the fourteen domains have never been tested:
biology, business, chemistry, economics, health and \emph{other}.
Chemistry is the interesting one --- it is one of only two domains
that reorder at all in the settled-roster screen
(Section~\ref{sec:res-law}). But the same refit shows the screen
saturating everywhere else, so additional domains at this roster are
additional replications of one measurement, and the honest reason to
run them is coverage, not the hope of a different answer. The
registered mathematics prediction remains open
(Appendix~\ref{app:preregs}): the settled batch includes a mathematics
run, but under a protocol the registration predates.

\item \textbf{Isolating the rubric text from its bundle.} The two arms
of the settled batch differ in system prompt, user scaffold, verdict
format and confidence handling at once
(Table~\ref{tab:arm-differences}), so the measured decisiveness
difference belongs to the bundle. One further condition separates
them: the generic system prompt run through the cell-scoped arm's own
template and parser, replaying the same triples. One domain suffices,
the replay machinery exists, and the cost is a few thousand judge
calls. This also subsumes the withdrawn pilot's rubric-free condition,
which was never run on working code.

\item \textbf{A roster clustered within about ten accuracy points.} The
granularity null of Section~\ref{sec:res-granularity} was measured on a
wide-spread roster where global capability ordering dominates by
construction. A tightly clustered roster is the version of
that question that could still come out positive, and it is untested.

\item \textbf{Generalisation to all 14 domains at matched statistical
power}, rather than a few selected domains at high power.

\item \textbf{A general trace-presence threshold in the corpus
validator.} The current exclusion policy is a curated list of ten model
names. A failure branch on the trace-presence check at, say, $0.90$
turns it into a threshold that generalises to models nobody has
inspected. It is a one-line change and it makes the limitation of
Section~\ref{subsec:disc-external-validity} actionable.

\item \textbf{A Davidson tie model.} Section~\ref{sec:res-tie-policy}
shows that the choice between half-weighting and dropping ties is not
neutral on this pipeline. Fitting tie propensity as a parameter
replaces a convention that currently changes conclusions with an
estimate that can be reported with an interval.

\item \textbf{A direct estimate of judge memorisation}, by verdict
consistency under paraphrased questions or by a probe set published
after the judge model's training cutoff. The answer-key-blind protocol
cannot defend against contamination that sits in the judge's weights.

\item \textbf{A held-out routing check.} Cell membership on held-out
data is what every arena result depends on, and it is the least
verified part of the pipeline: routing is soft, its calibration error
is exported under a defective aggregation, and no result in this thesis
tests it directly (Section~\ref{sec:arch-limits}). It sits last because
it changes no reported number, and first among the things that would
have to hold before any of them is deployed.

\end{enumerate}

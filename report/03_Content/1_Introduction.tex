\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

\subsection{The Routing Problem and the Agentic Turn}\label{subsec:the-agentic-turn}

Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly act inside larger software
pipelines rather than as interfaces that answer individual prompts.
Complex tasks are decomposed into sub-tasks and dispatched to
specialised tools and APIs, and the same
dispatch pattern extends to models themselves: HuggingGPT uses an LLM
controller to route each sub-task to a specialised
model. Systems built this way pursue goals across extended sequences of decisions, a transition termed the \textit{agentic turn}.
The quality of this dispatch depends on one question:
\textit{which model should handle a given sub-task?}

Answering it requires a fine-grained, up-to-date capability map of the
available models.
Agentic systems are increasingly deployed in
enterprise settings~\parencite{pwc2025survey}, yet the evaluation infrastructure
needed to build such a map remains coarse: mainstream benchmark
leaderboards report a single aggregate score per model, which conflates
performance across semantically heterogeneous tasks and gives no signal
about domain-specific strengths or failure modes, and the systems that
do induce finer structure are examined against this thesis's narrower
claims in Chapter~\ref{ch:literature-review}.
A routing decision based on a global score is a coarse
approximation to the question the orchestrating agent needs
answered.

This thesis builds and measures the substrate such a router would
consume: per-domain capability profiles at sufficient resolution to
distinguish models that are equal on average but opposite at the
margin.
It does not deploy a production router.

Stated in advance, the argument of the thesis is this. Systems that
dispatch tasks to different LLMs need to know which model is best
\emph{per topic}; leaderboards report one number per model. TaxoArena
splits an evaluation corpus into small, coherent topic cells, writes a
judging rubric for each cell, and has an LLM judge compare model
answers pairwise inside each cell --- a narrow cell makes a specific
rubric writable, and a specific rubric was supposed to make a better
judge.

Because the corpus has an answer key, every verdict can be checked,
and the checks show the judge does not apply the rubric: it verifies
which answer is correct, and a judge given no rubric at all reaches
the same winner on $99.4\%$ of comparisons. The partition has nothing
to reveal here either: under a pre-registered analysis, cells do not
carry different true model rankings on this corpus, so per-cell
evaluation cannot beat global evaluation no matter how good the cells
are. This thesis therefore set out to validate a taxonomy and ended by
measuring a judge, and the result is a boundary: wherever correctness
is checkable, a capable judge will shortcut the rubric, so on this
corpus cell-scoped judging had nothing left to improve; the setting
where it could matter --- open-ended tasks without a verifiable
key --- is exactly the routing setting, and the one this corpus
cannot test.

\subsection{The Evaluation Gap}\label{subsec:the-evaluation-gap}

Current evaluation frameworks do not distinguish model capabilities at
the resolution informed routing requires. The corpus is static, and
the score is a scalar per model, or per general domain (Math, Physics, Law, \ldots).

\subsubsection*{The Static Benchmark Problem}

Traditional benchmarks such as MMLU~\parencite{hendrycks2021measuring}
and its successor MMLU-Pro~\parencite{wang2024mmlupro} assess capability
via fixed, editorially curated question sets organised into predefined
subject categories.
As models improve, the hardest questions saturate: top-performing
models converge toward indistinguishable scores, and the benchmark
loses the ability to discriminate between closely performing
systems~\parencite{hendrycks2021measuring, wang2024mmlupro}.
The category structure is a second constraint, and it is the one this
thesis works on; it is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}.

\subsubsection*{The Scalar Fallacy and Aggregation Bias}

Even when evaluation signals are available, current ranking systems
reduce multidimensional capability profiles to a single scalar score,
such as an Elo rating or a weighted
accuracy~\parencite{boubdir2024elo}.
Averaging performance across all domains masks catastrophic failures in
specific subgroups and obscures profiles that differ in
composition while agreeing in mean~\parencite{raju2024constructing}.
Two models with identical global scores may differ sharply in their
domain profiles, one strong in formal reasoning and weak in applied
sciences and the other the reverse. A global leaderboard nonetheless
assigns them identical routing priority for every task, where a routing
policy should send them to entirely different sub-tasks.

$A$ and $B$. Every incoming question must be dispatched to one of
them. The only evidence the router holds is an evaluation of both
models on held-out questions, and the question is what resolution
that evaluation must have. The numbers that follow are synthetic,
constructed so that the failure mode is legible; they are not

The aggregate column is where the information disappears. Both models
score $0.72$, so a scalar leaderboard ranks them as interchangeable,
and a router that trusts it sends every question to whichever model
sits nominally first. Suppose that is $A$. Mathematics questions are
then served at $0.91$, which is correct, but History questions are
served at $0.55$ when $0.92$ was available. The wrong dispatch costs
$37$ points of accuracy on that domain; fixing on $B$ instead moves
the same loss to Mathematics. A router that reads the per-domain
columns pays this cost nowhere: it sends the integration problem
to $A$ and the source-interpretation question to $B$.
The aggregate score throws away exactly the information the routing
decision needs, and a benchmark adequate for routing must report
capability at the resolution of individual semantic sub-domains.
Figure~\ref{fig:routing-slopegraph} draws the same numbers as a

which is what a scalar ranking would require.
Per-domain rankings of real models do differ in
practice~\parencite{raju2024constructing}; whether a given roster
exhibits such crossings on a given corpus is an empirical question,
and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports that this corpus, for this
roster, does not.

\subsection{The Limits of Conventional Judging}\label{subsec:judging-limits}

Measuring performance on knowledge-intensive tasks requires a judge.
Expert human annotation remains the gold standard but is economically
prohibitive at the scale required for continuous, multi-model,
multi-domain evaluation~\parencite{zheng2023judging}.
Automated scalar scoring introduces systematic calibration errors,
because the judge has no stable internal reference for what a given
score means across domains, question types, or model generations.

Pairwise comparison resolves this by reducing judgement to a local,
contextualised question: is answer~A better than answer~B for this
specific query~\parencite{zheng2023judging}?
Yet LLM-as-a-judge systems exhibit biases even in pairwise mode:
\textit{positional bias} systematically favours the answer presented
first~\parencite{zheng2023judging}, \textit{verbosity bias} favours
longer answers regardless of quality~\parencite{chen2024verbosity},
and \textit{self-preference bias} favours answers that resemble the
judge's own outputs~\parencite{wataoka2025selfpreference}.
Beyond these, judges suffer from \emph{guideline incompleteness}: any
finite rubric under-specifies the evaluation space, leaving cases where
the judge falls back on undocumented heuristics.

\section{What a Partition Is For}\label{sec:problem-statement}

\textbf{A cell is a scope for a rubric.}
Splitting a cell reduces the surface over which a judge must
generalise. That is the entire mechanism.
A semantic partition of an evaluation corpus is usually presented as a
reporting device --- it decides which bucket each score is written
into --- and that is not what it is for here; the difference decides
what this thesis can claim.
A judge asked to compare two answers about ``Computer science'' must
hold one rubric that simultaneously covers operating systems,
cryptography, group theory and time-series diagnostics; whatever the
rubric says has to be true of all of them, so it can only say
domain-general things --- be correct, be clear, show your work.
A judge asked to compare two answers about ``Time Series Model
Diagnostics and Validation'' can be told what a good answer looks like
\emph{there}: which stationarity checks are load-bearing, which
residual diagnostics are decisive, which shortcuts are defensible.
The rubric becomes writable because the cell is narrow.
This is an argument, not a measurement, and it is the argument the
rest of the thesis tests.

\textbf{Why better prompting does not substitute for it.}
Guideline incompleteness is not a failure of prompt craft. A rubric
that must hold over a heterogeneous cell is under-specified by
construction: every criterion specific enough to discriminate is false
of part of the cell, so the criteria that survive the intersection are
the ones that discriminate least. Enlarging the prompt does not change
this; it changes which of the heterogeneous cases the extra text is
about. Narrowing the cell does change it, because it changes what the
intersection contains.

\textbf{What homogeneity is supposed to buy, stated as three
properties.}
First, a rubric becomes writable, in the sense above.
Second, pairwise outcomes within the cell become closer to
exchangeable, because the queries a comparison could have been drawn
from are similar to one another; the Bradley--Terry model that
aggregates the verdicts assumes a single latent strength per model
within the cell, and that assumption is a statement about the cell's
homogeneity rather than about the models.
Third, the per-cell ranking is allowed to differ from the global one,
which is the only way a capability \emph{profile} carries more
information than a capability \emph{score}.
The third property is the one that turns out to be empirical rather
than architectural, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports what happened
when it was tested.

\textbf{The four problems this leaves.}
The discussion above and in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} identifies
four unresolved problems, each a precondition for the next.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Fixed-corpus brittleness.}
    Existing benchmarks use editorially defined categories that do not
    adapt to the evolving capability space of LLMs.
    A framework that uses editorial labels as a scaffold but refines
    domain structure geometrically can discover capability dimensions
    the labels do not presuppose.

    \item \textbf{Scalar ranking and loss of capability resolution.}
    Aggregating performance into a single score conceals the domain
    structure of model capabilities, but routing requires per-domain
    rankings, not global leaderboards.

    \item \textbf{Incoherent domain boundaries.}
    Induced evaluation structure is well-defined only if the resulting
    cells are semantically precise and internally homogeneous, because
    coherence is what makes the rubric writable.
    Section~\ref{sec:arch-requirements} states this as a requirement
    with a threshold, rather than as an aspiration.

    \item \textbf{Judging at scale with controllable bias.}
    Automated evaluation must trade off calibration, bias, and domain
    sensitivity. Pairwise comparison with bidirectional order
    evaluation, domain-localised judging, and principled statistical
    aggregation can control the known biases while remaining tractable
    --- but only if the evaluation units are homogeneous enough that
    outcomes are exchangeable within each unit.

\end{enumerate}

\section{The System in Outline}\label{sec:approach}

TaxoArena works in three stages.
First, the 14 MMLU-Pro domain labels seed top-level anchor nodes, which
are then refined by embedding geometry: queries migrate to geometrically
closer anchors, and each anchor is recursively split into a tree of
semantically coherent leaves.
Second, each leaf induces a reference-informed judge rubric from
construction-set queries and their correct answers.
Third, held-out model responses are compared pairwise within leaves and
aggregated via a Bradley--Terry model~\parencite{bradley1952rank,
hunter2004mm} into per-domain capability profiles.

\textbf{Position of the taxonomy.}
The induced taxonomy is \textit{not} the contribution of this thesis; it is
the instrument by which the per-domain ranking resolution required for
routing is made available.
The construction is therefore presented in the main text at the level
needed to establish that the cells it produces are granular, coherent,
and stable enough for the arena to mean anything
(Section~\ref{sec:arch-requirements}); the formalism, the parameter
derivations and the fixed-point certificate are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:dag-formalism} and Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}.
This ordering is not a late concession. It is forced by a measurement:
an eleven-fold change in the number of cells, and a routing correction
worth $+10.3\%$ on the construction objective, both change the
system's measured discriminative power by zero
(Section~\ref{sec:res-granularity}). A construction whose tuning has no
measured evaluative consequence is an instrument, and it is documented
like one.

\textbf{Reference-informed at construction, answer-key-blind at test.}
Two properties of the evaluation design are central to its validity.
The \textit{judge-generation phase} uses correct MMLU-Pro answers from
the construction query set to induce domain-specific rubrics; the system
is \textit{reference-informed} at construction time.
The \textit{arena evaluation phase} judges held-out responses without
access to those queries' correct answers; the evaluation is
\textit{answer-key-blind} at test time, and the MMLU-Pro accuracy oracle
is revealed only after all matches are complete.
The separation is enforced in code by a hold-out filter that a build-time
assertion checks against the membership test itself
(Section~\ref{sec:arch-judge}).
Answer-key-blind is not the same as answer-blind, and the difference is
load-bearing for Chapter~\ref{ch:results}: the judge is shown the
multiple-choice options block, and $94.8\%$ of stored model traces state
their own selected answer.
The title term \textit{Dynamic Domains} refers to the dual property:
domain boundaries are seeded from MMLU-Pro labels and then adapted by
geometric evidence, so the evaluation dimensions reflect the structure
of the data rather than a fixed editorial taxonomy.
\textit{Query-Adaptive} names the corpus-level sense of that
adaptivity: the domain \emph{structure} adapts to the query corpus as
a whole --- corpus-adaptivity, distinct from the per-prompt sense in
which Prompt-to-Leaderboard conditions a full ranking on each
individual prompt~\parencite{frick2025p2l}.
\textit{Multidimensional} means the output is one ranking per cell
rather than one scalar per model.

\section{Three Links and the Research Questions}\label{sec:research-questions}

The claim that a partition improves evaluation is not one claim. It is a
chain of three, each separately falsifiable:

\begin{center}
coherent cells
$\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{(1)}\;}\;$ specific rubrics
$\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{(2)}\;}\;$ better judgments
$\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{(3)}\;}\;$ ranking closer to ground truth.
\end{center}

Link~1 asks whether the construction produces cells homogeneous enough
to write a rubric about. Link~2 asks whether cell-scoped rubrics are in
fact more specific than generic ones. Link~3 asks whether a judge
holding a specific rubric produces verdicts that track the truth more
closely than a judge holding a generic one.
Naming them separately is what makes the results of this thesis
readable, because its negative results land on different links and mean
different things there.

This decomposition was written after some of the results were in hand,
and specifically after the pre-registered granularity analysis
(Chapter~\ref{ch:results}) refuted the premise of the thesis's original
second research question. That is stated here rather than left to be
inferred: a reframing announced is a contribution, a reframing
concealed is a rationalisation.

The original second research question asked whether evaluation within
geometry-adapted leaf groupings improves ranking fidelity over flat
MMLU-Pro domain groupings, measured as
$\Delta\tau = \tau_{\text{adapted}} - \tau_{\text{canonical}}$ per cell.
Read literally that is a question about link~3's \emph{output}. The
pre-registered analysis reported in Section~\ref{sec:res-granularity}
found that the premise underneath it does not hold on this corpus:
cells do not carry different true model rankings, at any granularity
from 14 to 152 cells, under either router. A per-cell $\Delta\tau$
between adapted and canonical groupings therefore measures a difference
with no room to exist, and a null there says nothing about the
mechanism. The question is replaced rather than repaired.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] \emph{What decides the verdicts} of
    answer-key-blind, domain-conditioned pairwise LLM judgments ---
    and do those judgments recover per-domain capability rankings on
    held-out queries?
    \textit{Primary metric: agreement between the judge's winner and
    the MMLU-Pro answer key, per verdict. Spearman $\rho$ against
    ground-truth accuracy is reported alongside, under both tie
    policies, with its quantisation stated.}

    \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Does adapted, cell-scoped grouping enable
    \emph{better judging} than judging the same questions under a
    generic, partition-free rubric?
    \textit{Primary metric: the paired contrast between the two arms on
    an identical question set --- Spearman $\rho$ against ground-truth
    accuracy for the partition claim, per-verdict answer-key agreement
    for the judge-side check.}

\end{enumerate}

The second question is answerable with the same arena, and it is the
question the system actually poses. The difference from the original
wording matters for what gets reported: agreement between judge and
ground truth \emph{within} a grouping, not rank divergence
\emph{between} groupings.

\textbf{A convention for evidence.}
This thesis marks every substantive claim as either
\textsc{measured} --- there is data behind it, and it could have come
out otherwise --- or \textsc{argued} --- it is reasoning the thesis owes
the reader, with no data behind it. Motivation is argued; results are
measured; the two are not interchangeable and are not typeset as though
they were. Where a number exists but could not be reproduced under a
stated convention, it is reported as unresolved rather than quoted.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:research-goal}

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{A measured account of what an LLM judge decides, on a
    corpus with a verifiable answer key.} \textsc{measured}.
    On multiple-choice items the judge's verdict tracks answer
    correctness, not reasoning quality: it selects the correct response
    on the large majority of items where exactly one response is correct,
    its tie rate roughly triples on precisely those items where the key
    stops discriminating, and its agreement with the key is
    \emph{highest} where neither response carries any reasoning at all.
    Four controls support the decomposition, each sharing every confound
    with its treatment except the one being varied
    (Section~\ref{sec:res-judge}).

    \item \textbf{A pre-registered no-partition baseline, and what
    the registered rule returned.} \textsc{measured}.
    A paired arm judged an identical Math question set under
    cell-scoped rubrics and under a generic MT-Bench-style prompt with
    no partition at all. The reading rule, the decisive difference and
    the void conditions were fixed in writing before the comparison
    existed. The arms are indistinguishable on the high-power
    agreement metric; the registered decision rule returned a verdict
    for the partition-free arm on the low-power rank-correlation
    contrast, an outcome attributed --- after
    the fact, and marked as such --- to tie scoring
    (Section~\ref{sec:res-c5}).

    \item \textbf{A construction method presented as an instrument,
    with three guarantees and three named limitations.}
    \textsc{measured}.
    A certified structural fixed point, an acceptance gate calibrated in
    units of its own objective's paired standard error, and a separation
    threshold positioned between two measured nulls
    (Section~\ref{sec:arch-requirements}, Appendix~\ref{app:dag-formalism}).

    \item \textbf{A pre-registered negative result about
    geometry-adapted evaluation partitioning, scoped by power and by
    roster.} \textsc{measured}.
    Cells do not carry different true model rankings on this corpus for
    this roster, at any granularity tested. The three possible outcomes
    were named in advance, and the primary statistic was fixed in
    advance --- which is the only reason the result is readable, since
    the secondary statistic moves the other way
    (Section~\ref{sec:res-granularity}).

    \item \textbf{The reframing itself.} \textsc{argued}.
    The evaluative value of a taxonomy is a claim about a judge's
    generalisation surface, and it decomposes into three separately
    testable links. This thesis measures link~1 directly, link~2 against
    a randomised null, and link~3 at the premise level.

\end{enumerate}

Contribution~1 is the one that changed. The thesis set out to validate a
taxonomy and ended by measuring a judge. That is stated here rather than
in the conclusion, because it is the reason
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} leads with the judge and not with the partition.
An active matchmaking scheduler keeps judge-call cost tractable, but it
supports the arena rather than standing as a contribution
(Section~\ref{sec:meth-scheduling}).

\section{Scope and Validity}\label{sec:intro-scope}

The arena results are a selected-domain study, not a global validation.
The reported runs evaluate two domains (Math, and law, the domain an
offline screen selects) at high statistical power rather than
spreading the same judge-call budget across all 14 MMLU-Pro domains;
the selection rule is specified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental-design}, the screen in
Section~\ref{sec:res-law}, and replications on philosophy and history
are launched with results pending.

Three scope conditions are stated in advance because they bound
everything downstream.
First, MMLU-Pro is multiple-choice with a verifiable key. This is what
makes the comparison against ground truth possible, and it is
simultaneously the property that lets a capable judge bypass the rubric
by verifying the answer. Both are true, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} treats the second as a measurement of where
the architecture binds rather than as a defect.
Second, the pilot arena run carries a data defect: response text was not
persisted for four of its eight models, so half of its comparisons were
decided by which side had text at all. The defect is reported as a
result, with what it qualifies and what survives it stated explicitly
(Section~\ref{sec:res-capture-gap}).
Third, the reported rank correlations are quantised and, at this model
count, not stable to the third decimal across defensible analysis
conventions. Paired contrasts within a run are safe; absolute values
quoted to three decimals are not, and the thesis does not quote them
that way (Section~\ref{sec:res-tie-policy}).

The evaluation's validity rests on the reference-informed,
answer-key-blind separation defined in Section~\ref{sec:approach}.
The three-way construction / tuning-validation / arena-test partition
and the information-separation protocol that enforce it are specified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental-design}.

\section{Thesis Structure}\label{sec:thesis-structure}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature-review} gives the background the argument
uses: benchmark saturation, what is known to go wrong with LLM judges,
pairwise ranking and Bradley--Terry estimation, the family the
construction belongs to, and query routing as the consumer of the
output.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} is the method. It opens by stating
what a partition must satisfy for the arena to mean anything, and shows
that the induced partition satisfies it; it then specifies the arena ---
the judge, the estimator, the scheduler, and an inventory of what is
\emph{not} implemented --- and closes with the measurement rules the
thesis holds itself to.
Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental-design} fixes the corpus, the rosters, the
conditions, the pre-registrations with their dates, and the statistical
protocol.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports what was measured, in mechanism order:
first what the judge does, then what the partition does.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets those results, states where the
architecture binds, records the errors the apparatus caught in its own
published numbers, and concludes.
